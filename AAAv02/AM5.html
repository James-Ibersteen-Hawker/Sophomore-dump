AM-5 Mortar Tank

The Reichenbach AM-5 Self-Propelled Mortar Tank, designed by Reichenbach TDI (Tank Development Industry) is a second-generation super heavy tank, though the current AM-5E is a fourth-generation tank, designed primarily for use as a siege tank, but could also be deployed as a mobile fort and/or troop support tank. The largest and heaviest tank ever built, it is armed with six single-fire 1000mm mortars and four 12.7mm Browning machine guns, giving it the most firepower of any tank for its size and weight. Since its introduction into service, it has performed a major role in tactical and strategic engagements, leveling and storming through enemy defenses with ease.

Early Years

First conceived in 1942 by Kurt Reichenbach, the design was considered radical for its time. Instead of mounting a cannon or mortar into a separate turret, like most tanks did, this tank instead had the mortars built into the tank itself. The tank's armor was 600mm thick, thicker than anything similar that had been proposed. This was due to the fact that the tank would be largely motionless on the battlefield, making it easy prey to enemy fire. The cabin was elevated off of the ground and could be accessed either by a hatch located on the top of the tank, or through a small door on either side. The design was first pitched to the Wehrmacht in the year 1943. Werner Eduard Fritz von Blomberg, the current Minister of War in Germany, was interested. However, due to the fact that Kurt was relatively new and inexperienced when it came to building tanks and ground vehicles, his only other concept being the twin-barreled AM-2 tank, and the pressing monetary restrictions of the ongoing war, no funding was forthcoming.

Immigration to the US

After the conclusion of WWII, Kurt fled to the United States, whereupon he immediately applied for citizenship despite his refugee status and offered up all of his knowledge of tank-building to the military in exchange for being hired as chief executive over said knowledge. After two years of negotiation, the decision was finally made by Secretary of War Robert P. Patterson to promote Kurt to the position of senior technician in exchange for his two previous tank designs. Kurt was immediately granted citizenship by the government and set to work refining his AM-5 design, refitting it with new armor plates and electronics that weren't available when he had first pitched it to the Wehrmacht. Within one year he pitched it to the U.S. Army and was granted 1,000,000 USD for the construction of a full-scale mockup, as well as a third-scale mockup. The third-scale mockup was completed in December of 1948 and it was sent to the Army for stress-testing immediately. Due to ongoing metal shortages, the full-scale mockup would not be completed for another two years, and by that point Kurt had already gone over-budget by 50,000 USD. However, when it was completed, the military assessed the design and determined that it had potential. The project was designated XMT-5, standing for Experimental Mortar Tank no.5, and an initial order was placed for two prototypes.



Assassination

Unfortunately, in the spring of 1951, Kurt was assassinated, presumably by German spies sent to kill him for revealing German war secrets to the U.S. military. No assassin was ever apprehended, and one month later the development of the XMT-5 was cancelled. Angry and drunk, Kurt's elder brother Oscar Reichenbach, who had also escaped from Germany after the end of WWII, stated in a bet of 5,000 USD that he would finish Kurt's work.



Rebirth

With 5,000 USD at stake, Oscar went to Kurt's associates in the military and asked them for support to help start what Oscar called the Reichenbach Development Center, or the RDC. Together with head engineer Sean McGallin, the pair set up shop in an old warehouse which contained the abandoned mockup of the XMT-5, as well as the plans. Together, along with another associate of Kurt's, senior technician Mike D. Grasse, they spent five years building a functional prototype which they named the Heiliger von Kurt (Saint of Kurt), which rolled out under its own power in 1957.



Design

The AM-5 consists of a 6 meter, or roughly 20 feet, long stretched-octagonal shell, with two central chassis powering the four treads, each of which is 18 meters long and ¾ of a meter wide. The cockpit of the tank is located in the far front of the body of the tank, with large, bombproof windows yielding high forward visiblity, as well as two smaller 'forehead' window-canopies, allowing the 3 operators to see upwards. Into the back of the tank were sunk six wells, three on each side. In each of these wells there is a large, 3 tonne steel-reinforced ceramic crucible with a diameter of 1000mm, or approximately 1 meter. This crucible is connected to the sides of the well via eight springs, which are mounted on special mounting points on the crucible. The upper four connect to the opening of the well, and the bottom four connect the the floor of the well. At the bottom of the crucible, there is a carbon-fiber pipe which connects to a hole at the bottom of the well, providing an escape for the exhaust gases.

The mortars can fire a range of shells, going from 3 ton hollow 'flare shells' which explode into brilliant fireworks after being fired, to the 20 ton concrete shells, a variant of which can be filled with 10 tons of high explosives. In addition, the tank's wide barrels allow for the launching of small 300mm rockets, which can be armed with high explosives or can also be armed with disposable cameras to make for primitive surveillance drones.

Each of the mortars, due to their orientation, cannot be reloaded in the field and only have a single shot before having to cool down for at least fifteen minutes.
